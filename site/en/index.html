Earliest swapping for every chain length: Kamin et al. (2023), Section IV D 1, Eq. (41)

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

When neighboring parts of a quantum communication chain become ready at different times, joining them in different orders can change the damage caused by waiting. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang argue that always choosing the adjacent pair that can be joined earliest minimizes this damage for every chain length, regardless of how ties are broken.

The model starts with a row of nonnegative real numbers, one readiness time per elementary link. Joining two neighboring entries costs their absolute difference and replaces them by their maximum: the combined block cannot be ready before both parts are ready. A complete history repeats this until one entry remains; its total cost represents dephasing, the loss of quantum coherence from waiting in this model. The question concerns the best order for a fixed row, with deterministic, instantaneous joins, called swaps. According to the manuscript, Kamin, Shchukin, Schmidt and van Loock conjectured this earliest-swapping rule in 2023 and verified it through length eight. The authors supply a written argument for every length and nonnegative real readiness time.

Their central device is a number assigned to every intermediate row, called a potential. Take the largest entry, add the maximum of each neighboring pair, and subtract the sum of all entries. The authors identify this number with the minimum possible remaining cost. It is also half the sum of the absolute changes between neighboring times and the waiting contributions from both endpoints up to the largest time. This connects it to the noise expression of Goodenough, Coopmans and Towsley. Their combined exponent equals twice this potential under the respective normalizations; neighboring differences alone describe a different objective.

Why does the potential prove optimality? For any swap, its immediate cost plus the potential afterward equals the potential beforehand plus a nonnegative excess, or slack. This slack measures increases in the neighboring-pair maxima immediately outside the merged pair; replacement by the larger entry makes each increase nonnegative. If the chosen pair has the smallest maximum, comparison with its neighboring pairs shows that neither outside maximum increases. Thus every earliest swap has zero slack, including tied choices and endpoint swaps. Adding these identities through a complete history cancels intermediate potentials. The final singleton has potential zero, so total cost equals initial potential plus accumulated slack. Consequently no history beats the initial potential, earliest swapping attains it, and a history is optimal exactly when all its slacks vanish.

Some non-greedy histories are therefore also optimal. In the manuscript’s example, the times are (1, 2, 4, 3). Joining the last two first costs one, although their readiness maximum is four rather than the earliest available two. The remaining row is (1, 2, 4); joining its first pair costs one, and the final join costs two. The total four equals the potential, so this non-greedy history is optimal. For increasing or decreasing readiness times, the formula simplifies to largest minus smallest. The authors also show that greedy merge times never decrease, so the rule respects readiness, including consecutive instantaneous swaps at a tied time.

The pointwise comparison has probabilistic consequences without independence or identical distributions. For the same realized readiness row, earliest swapping costs no more than any competing history, even one selected with additional randomness. It minimizes expected cost when costs are integrable and maximizes the expectation of e^(−αC) for every nonnegative α, where C is total cost and α measures attenuation strength. The exponential comparison needs no moment assumption because these factors lie between zero and one. This answers Kamin and colleagues’ mean-versus-exponential question within the stated recurrence. For independent geometric readiness times with a common success probability, the authors also derive an explicit mean optimum using expectations of the overall maximum, neighboring maxima and individual times.

The manuscript distinguishes its evidence. It cites pinned Lean source establishing the natural-number version, including arbitrary greedy ties and zero costs for empty and singleton lists. The real-valued-time extension, slack characterization and probabilistic consequences have written proofs here, not claimed Lean coverage. It also describes exact finite Python checks on bounded integer and rational grids, distinct-rank permutations and selected geometric parameters. These complement rather than replace the general proof; describing the cited evidence does not certify it.

The particular gain is a closed formula and a local explanation of all optimal histories, replacing earlier finite-length evidence with the authors’ all-length result. Its scope remains fixed-readiness, deterministic, instantaneous swapping. Optimizing distribution schedules, memory cutoffs, probabilistic swaps, or secret-key rates across schemes changes the problem. The authors explicitly do not settle those broader questions.
